# How the brain gets smarter

**Screening literature without reading all of it · separating fact from lore ·
what can improve autonomously and what must never.**

---

## 1. The literature list

`LITERATURE.md`, generated by `tools/bibliography.py`. Everything STAN holds,
grouped by state, with what each item may and may not be cited for, and a
reading queue ordered by what unblocks the most.

Regenerate it whenever the register changes. Never edit it by hand.

**Screened-out sources stay in the list.** A register showing what was
considered and rejected — with the reason — is worth far more than one showing
only survivors. It is also the difference between "we looked and this does not
apply" and "we never looked", which is exactly what a clinician will want to
know.

## 2. Screening — deciding without reading the whole thing

Reading every paper in full is impossible and unnecessary. Most can be settled
in under two minutes. The discipline is having explicit criteria and recording
the decision.

### Stage 1 — Title. Five seconds.

Does this plausibly bear on a question in the bank? If not, screened out.
Record the reason in one clause. No judgement calls at this stage — when in
doubt, promote it.

### Stage 2 — Abstract. Two minutes. Five questions.

**Who did they study?** The single most important thing, and the commonest
failure. Men in their sixties with age-related decline are a different
population from a thirty-two-year-old four years off 400mg a week. A source can
be excellent and still say nothing about our readers.

**Which catalogued question does this bear on?** Name it. If it bears on none,
it is interesting rather than useful — park it rather than reading it.

**What could this design actually establish?** A cross-sectional study cannot
show causation. A trial with forty people cannot rule out an uncommon harm. An
animal study is mechanism, never outcome.

**Was it designed to answer our question, or does our question ride on a
secondary outcome?** Secondary outcomes are hypothesis-generating. Papers get
over-cited for findings their authors never powered the study to detect.

**Can we get the full text?** No point queueing what nobody can open.

Decision: **read in full · park · screen out**, with a recorded reason.

### Stage 3 — Methods and results. Ten minutes. Not the discussion.

For anything that survives Stage 2, read the methods and the tables **before**
the discussion or conclusion.

This ordering is deliberate and it matters more than it sounds. Authors'
conclusions routinely outrun their own data, and an abstract's closing sentence
is where that overreach lives. Read the conclusion first and you are anchored to
it — you then read the results looking for confirmation, which is the same
failure that produced STAN's first scrapped content sweep, just one level up.

What to extract at this stage: sample size, who was excluded, how exposure was
measured (self-reported AAS history is not a measurement), what was actually
compared, and confidence intervals rather than p-values.

### Stage 4 — Full read, and extraction.

Only what survives. This is where claims get made.

### Recording it

Screening decisions live on the source record: `review.status` gains
`screened-out`, and `notes` carries the reason. Cheap to do, and it means the
same paper is never assessed twice.

## 3. Fact and lore, kept apart

Two registers, never mixed:

| | **Claim** | **Belief** |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A statement traceable to a quote in a source that was read | A statement widely held in the community |
| May support an answer | Yes | **Never** |
| May be reported | Yes | Yes — *as a belief*, with its status shown |
| Its evidential status | Established by the source | **Is itself the finding** |

Schema at `knowledge/schema/belief.schema.yaml`.

Discarding community knowledge would be wrong twice over: it is frequently the
only information anyone has, and it is what people are acting on tonight.
Presenting it as fact would be wrong too. So it gets recorded, named, and held
against the evidence — with `untested` expected to be the commonest verdict, and
treated as a genuine finding rather than an absence.

Every belief carries **`what_would_settle_it`** — the study that would answer it
— and **`harm_if_wrong`**, which is how the list gets ranked. An untested belief
that is harmless if wrong can wait. One that is not, cannot.

## 4. Why the bodybuilding community would actually come

They will not come for "steroids are bad." They will come for four things, and
STAN can offer all four without compromising anything.

**Numbers, taken seriously.** This is a community that reads its own bloodwork,
argues about half-lives and tracks everything. Meet it at that level. The
encyclopedia and the panel-reading material are written for people who want
detail, not reassurance.

**No moralising.** Already in `SCOPE.md`: we do not tell anyone what they can
and cannot do. That single commitment is worth more than any amount of outreach.

**Settling the arguments.** Forums litigate the same questions endlessly — does
an AI actually help, is hCG worth running, does anything meaningfully speed
recovery — with enormous confidence and no data. Whoever can say what has
actually been tested becomes the referee.

**The belief register itself.** A page reading *"twelve things widely believed:
three supported, two contradicted, seven never studied"* is the most shareable
thing STAN could publish. It respects what the community knows, is completely
honest about what is established, and exists nowhere else.

That page runs in both directions. To the community it says: you are not being
lectured, and some of what you believe holds up. To a researcher it says: here
is a ranked list of unanswered questions with a population already engaged and
waiting. Very few patient organisations can hand an academic that.

## 5. Breadth of evidence

The source taxonomy now spans what the questions actually require: randomised
trials, cohort and case-control studies, registries, surveys, case reports,
**animal studies**, **in-vitro work** and **pharmacokinetics**, alongside
guidelines, systematic reviews and textbooks.

Two standing rules on the wider material:

- **Animal and in-vitro work is citable for mechanism, never for human
  outcome.** Every such record must say so in `population_relevance`. It
  explains *how* something might work; it never establishes *that* it does in
  people.
- **Type is not weight.** A well-conducted cohort study in the right population
  beats a randomised trial in the wrong one. `type` says what kind of thing it
  is; `evidence_level.grade` and `population_relevance` say what it can bear.

The relevant fields are broader than endocrinology alone. Cardiology owns the
consequences people die of; pharmacology owns half-lives, esters, first-pass
metabolism and everything about how a compound behaves; hepatology, haematology,
psychiatry and sports medicine all hold pieces. Topics are indexed accordingly
rather than filed under one specialty.

## 6. Autonomous improvement — what is real

The honest split. Discovery and prioritisation can run on their own. Assertion
cannot, and must not.

### What can run without a human

**New literature surveillance.** PubMed's E-utilities API is free and public.
Standing queries per topic, run weekly; anything new lands in the screening
queue. This alone means the register stops depending on someone remembering to
go looking.

**Retraction and correction watch.** PubMed flags both. Any held source that is
retracted or corrected raises an alert against every claim and answer that used
it. This is not a nicety — citing a retracted paper is the kind of error that
ends an organisation's credibility permanently.

**Supersession detection.** A guideline reaching a new edition; a systematic
review appearing that includes a source we hold. Both mean re-reading.

**Search variant learning.** Unmet queries that semantically match an existing
question get proposed as new variants. Every week the search understands more
phrasings, without a single new source. **This is the compounding one.**

**Question discovery.** Clusters of unmet queries matching nothing become
proposed questions.

**Backlog re-ranking.** Unanswered questions reorder themselves by how often
they are actually being asked, so effort follows demand rather than intuition.

**Review scheduling and coverage reporting.** Already built.

### What must never run without a human

- Extracting a claim without someone verifying the quote against the document
- Publishing any answer
- Approving anything clinical
- Changing what a source is recorded as supporting
- Setting a belief's status

The line is exactly the one in `QA-ENGINE.md`: **the machine may propose;
only a person may assert.** A system that improves its own assertions
autonomously is the thing that would destroy this project, and it would do so
invisibly and at scale.

### Three kinds of getting smarter

Worth separating, because only one is blocked on documents:

| | Improves by | Needs |
|---|---|---|
| **Breadth** | More sources, more claims | Documents. The current bottleneck. |
| **Precision** | Better matching, more variants | Traffic. No new papers required. |
| **Self-knowledge** | Better at naming its own gaps | Logs. No new papers required. |

Two of the three compound without a single new source. A hundred people
searching and finding nothing makes the system measurably better at
understanding the next hundred — provided the logging exists to catch it.

## 7. What to build, in order

1. **`LITERATURE.md`** — done, generated.
2. **Screening fields** — add `screened-out` to source status and a screening
   note. Small.
3. **PubMed surveillance** — standing queries, weekly, into a screening queue.
   Free API, modest work, and it is what stops the register going stale.
4. **Retraction watch** — same API. Higher value than it looks.
5. **Variant learning** — needs the search log, which needs a server.
6. **Belief register** — start recording as they come up in conversation and in
   groups. Needs no sources to begin.

Items 3 and 4 are the ones that make the phrase "gets smarter over time" mean
something concrete rather than aspirational.
