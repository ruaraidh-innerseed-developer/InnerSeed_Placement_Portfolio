# STAN — strategic assessment

**Written at the founder's request: is this worth pursuing, what is missing, and
what is the best realistic outcome.** Blunt by design.

---

## 1. Where it actually stands

| | State |
|---|---|
| Governance | Complete. Arguably over-complete for an organisation of one. |
| Engineering | Complete for a prototype. 109 routable pages, generated from data, enforced by a validator. |
| Content | **Zero** backed answers. 68 questions catalogued, 9 sources held, none read. |
| People | **One.** No second reader, no clinician, no facilitator. |
| Public presence | **None.** The prototype is a private link. |
| Revenue | **None**, and no mechanism built for any. |

The ratio is stark: several thousand lines of apparatus, no substance, no people, no
public. This is not a criticism of the apparatus — it is genuinely what makes STAN
defensible in a field where nothing else is. But it has been true for weeks and no
amount of further building changes it. **The binding constraint is human, and it
has not moved.**

## 2. Is it worth pursuing?

**Yes — but not in its current shape.**

The case for is strong and has not weakened:

- The gap is real and *published*. Grant et al. 2023 documents that endocrinologists
  have no consistent approach. That is the gap in a peer-reviewed paper.
- The population is large, under-served, and — this matters — **affluent in the
  relevant sense.** People who spend hundreds a month on compounds and bloodwork are
  not price-sensitive about their own health once someone credible offers to help.
  The private TRT sector proves the willingness to pay; it just serves the wrong
  segment.
- Nobody credible occupies the space. It is affiliate-funded forums at one end and
  private clinics that do not understand PED use at the other. The middle is empty.
- Lived experience plus real governance is a combination nobody else has.
- Harm reduction is an established, fundable field in the UK with existing
  commissioners and existing academic allies.

The case against is real and should not be waved away:

- Solo founder, weeks in, still nobody else involved. Every risk below compounds
  from that one.
- The current design is a **library with a groups annex.** Libraries do not generate
  income and do not, on their own, retain people.
- "Non-profit that must generate profit" is an unresolved tension in the founding
  concept, and unresolved tensions in founding concepts do not resolve themselves.
- The legal surface — advice versus information, prescription-only medicines,
  safeguarding — is genuinely tricky and has only been addressed on paper.

**Verdict:** the opportunity is real and the apparatus is sound. What is missing is a
business, a second person, and a public front door. In that order of difficulty,
reversed: the front door is a weekend, the second person is a conversation, the
business is the rest of this document.

## 3. The reframe: STAN is a service, not a hub

The founder's phrase — **"income through traded time"** — is the key that has been
missing, and it reframes the whole project.

A resource hub sells nothing. A support network with paid tiers sells *time*: a
peer's, a facilitator's, a clinician's. That is a service business, and service
businesses in health have a well-understood shape:

```
   FREE            LOW COST            MID                 HIGH              RECURRING
   ────            ────────            ───                 ────              ─────────
   Q&A hub         Peer session        Bloods package      Clinical consult  Monitoring
   Open groups     Cohort (6 wk)       + peer walkthrough  via STAN panel    membership
   Crisis routing
   ↓               ↓                   ↓                   ↓                 ↓
   builds trust    proves people pay   the wedge           the margin        the moat
```

The hub — the thing all the engineering has gone into — is the **top of the funnel**,
not the product. It is how people arrive and why they trust you. It is not what they
pay for.

### What people would actually pay for

**The man who has stopped and feels wrong.** Frightened, dismissed by his GP, told
his bloods are normal. Will pay for: an hour with someone who has been there, a
clinician who will not judge him, a structured six weeks with others in the same
state. Willingness to pay is high. A £45 peer session is less than a week of what he
was spending on compounds.

**The man staying on who wants to do it less stupidly.** The *largest* segment and
the *least* served. Private TRT clinics want "low T" men on 100mg a week; nobody
wants to work with the man on 500mg who is not stopping. He will pay — recurringly —
for monitoring, interpretation and a clinician who will actually engage. This is the
segment the entire private sector is refusing, which is exactly why it is the
opportunity.

**Partners and family.** Lower willingness to pay but real, and nobody serves them
at all.

**Clinics.** The B2B side. A vetted clinic pays for qualified referrals — people who
arrive pre-educated, know what they need, and convert. This is where the margin is,
and it is compatible with integrity provided the fee is published on the referral
page, which the scope document already requires.

**Commissioners.** Slow, grant-shaped, but real: harm reduction services and
training are things public health bodies fund. This is the charity side's income,
not the trading side's.

## 4. What is missing — five things

### 4.1 The service ladder

The site describes information, groups and "connect" and then stops. It needs the
ladder above, made visible, with the free tier genuinely free and each paid step
clearly what it is and what it costs. Not because everyone will climb it — most will
not — but because a site with a ladder reads as a service and a site without one
reads as a leaflet.

### 4.2 A wedge product — deliverable now, needs no clinician

**The bloods walkthrough.** Forty-five minutes, booked, paid. A man brings his panel.
A trained peer — initially the founder — walks through what each marker *is*, helps
him fill in the consultation brief, and sends him to his appointment able to ask the
right questions.

Why this one:

- **Deliverable this month.** Needs the founder, a calendar link, and a payment link.
- **Tier A.** Explaining what a marker is and helping someone organise their own
  results and formulate questions is education and preparation, not interpretation.
  It stays on the right side of the line *provided* the framing is disciplined: what
  the markers are, not what his numbers mean.
- **Feeds the funnel.** Every walkthrough ends with "and here is who can actually
  interpret this" — which becomes a clinical referral the moment the panel exists.
- **Proves the thesis.** If men will not pay £45 for this, the rest of the model
  needs rethinking. Better to learn that in month one.

**The line to hold:** the walkthrough teaches him to read a panel; it does not read
*his* panel. That distinction is the whole difference between a service and a
liability, and the peer running it needs to hold it in the room, not just on paper.

### 4.3 The clinical panel

The single most valuable asset STAN could build. Not employees — associates. Three
to five clinicians who understand this population, who take STAN referrals, and whom
STAN has vetted. The user gets seen by someone who will not judge him; the clinician
gets patients they would otherwise never reach; STAN takes a published fee.

This is what turns "signposting" — sending people away — into "referral" — sending
people *through*. Signposting away loses the user and earns nothing. Referral
through keeps the relationship and funds the free tier.

The outreach pack to Jayasena's group was written to recruit a *reviewer*. It should
be recruiting *panel members*, and the ask changes: not "would you check this page"
but "would you see people we send you." That is a far more interesting proposition
to a clinician, because it is patients rather than homework.

### 4.4 The founder's role, corrected

Earlier guidance said the founder should not be the service. That was right for the
crisis line and wrong for the business. **Every service business starts with the
founder as the service.** The rule is not "never be the service"; it is "do not be
the *only* service forever, and never be the one holding a crisis alone."

Run the walkthroughs. Facilitate the first cohort. Be the face of the seminars.
That is how the thing gets proven and how the founder gets paid. Then hire.

### 4.5 The entity, resolved

"Non-profit that generates profit" is not a contradiction; it is a structure, and a
standard one:

```
   CHARITY / CIC                     TRADING COMPANY (Ltd)
   ─────────────                     ─────────────────────
   Holds: mission, brand, content,   Runs: walkthroughs, cohorts, clinical
          free groups, crisis work          bookings, referral fees, training
   Funded by: grants, donations,     Funded by: revenue
              commissioning
   Pays: nobody much                 Pays: the founder a salary. Staff.
   Receives: surplus from trading    Gifts: surplus to the charity
             company
```

The founder is employed by the trading company and paid from revenue. The charity
holds the trust and receives the surplus. Half the UK charity sector works this way.
It resolves the tension entirely, and it means the free tier is funded by the paid
tier rather than by the founder's goodwill.

**One honest note:** the non-profit framing was partly this assistant's suggestion
early on, for credibility. Credibility actually comes from the governance and the
clinicians, not from the legal form. If the two-entity structure feels heavy, a
mission-locked Ltd is a legitimate alternative and simpler to run. The charity side
mainly matters if grant and commissioner income is a serious goal.

## 5. Best realistic outcome

**Year one.** Public site live with the wedge service running. Fifty to two hundred
paying walkthroughs. First cohort completed. One clinician on the panel — probably a
pharmacist or an andrology registrar rather than a professor. Founder part-time,
not yet paid from it. The unmet-search log has a thousand entries and is the content
plan.

**Year two.** Panel of three to five. Cohorts running continuously. Referral income
covering hosting and a part-time facilitator. Founder drawing a modest salary from
the trading company. First grant application in, on the strength of a year of real
usage data. Seminars recorded and in the library.

**Year three.** STAN is the name a GP gives when a patient mentions steroids. Gym
owners know it. Clinics want to be on the panel. Grant income funds the free tier;
commercial income funds staff. A commissioner conversation is plausible.

**The ceiling.** The front door for PED-related health in the UK — the one
organisation that both the man on 500mg and the consultant endocrinologist
consider legitimate. Nobody occupies that position. It is available.

**The floor.** A beautifully governed, empty repository, because the founder never
recruited a second person. This is the most likely outcome if nothing changes, and
it is entirely avoidable.

## 6. The single determining factor

Not the technology. Not the content. Not the model.

**Whether a second human is involved within a month.**

Every other risk compounds from solo founding. Every unlock — publishing, the
wedge, the panel, the entity — needs someone else in the room. The apparatus is
finished. The next commit that matters is not code.

## 7. Immediate sequence

1. **Public URL and a STAN email address.** A weekend. Gates everything.
2. **Recruit a second reader.** One person, one hour a week. Unblocks Tier A.
3. **Set up the walkthrough.** Calendar link, payment link, the consultation brief
   as the deliverable. Founder runs it. Price it at £45.
4. **Send the first outreach email** — reframed as "would you see people we send
   you" rather than "would you review a page."
5. **Choose the entity.** The two-entity structure is the recommendation.
   Jurisdiction still unknown after six weeks; it affects the charity side only.
6. **Get the free chapters into the sources inbox.** The content engine has been
   waiting on this since the day it was built.

Items 1 to 4 are a fortnight. None of them is engineering.
