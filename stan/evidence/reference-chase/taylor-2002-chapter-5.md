# Reference chase — Taylor 2002, Chapter 5

**Source:** `taylor-2002-anabolic-steroids-athlete` · *Anabolic Steroids and the
Athlete*, 2nd ed., McFarland 2002 · Chapter 5, "Anabolic Steroid Regimes Used
by Athletes", pp. 59–64.

A secondary source is worth most for the primary sources it points at. Chapter 5
carries twenty-six numbered references and every one of them is a paper STAN
may want. Chasing them turns one book on a table into a reading list, and the
papers — not the book — are what STAN will actually cite.

**What is needed to fill this in:** a photograph of the endnotes for Chapter 5.
In McFarland books these sit either at the foot of the chapter or in a notes
section at the back, numbered by chapter. Six lines of small print, one photo,
and this table stops being empty.

## Why the priority order

**Ref 12 first.** The claim resting on it is the one most likely to be
misread as reassurance. The passage lists blood pressure, lipoproteins and
liver enzymes as returning to normal, and does *not* list testosterone, LH or
FSH. Whether that omission is the study's or the author's summary of it cannot
be known without the paper. Until it is read, STAN cannot safely say anything
about it — and someone who has stopped and feels wrong is exactly the person
who would be hurt by getting this one wrong.

**Refs 13 and 14 second.** These sit under the most-repeated claim in the
bodybuilding community — that anabolic steroids permanently add myonuclei, so
muscle comes back faster ever after. The 2002 sentence does not say that. It
says the counts increase. Whether the studies were human or rodent decides
whether STAN can write about humans at all, and the field has moved
substantially since, so these two get a current literature search alongside
them rather than instead of them.

**Refs 1–11 third**, because the claim they support is one STAN concedes
rather than contests, and eleven references is a large chase for a point
nobody disputes. Worth doing to find the two or three best, not all eleven.

## Working notes

- Several of these will be behind paywalls. Record what is inaccessible in the
  source record's notes rather than quietly dropping it — a register that shows
  what could not be obtained is worth more than one that shows only what could.
- Anything from the 1980s and 1990s needs its population read carefully.
  Much of the era's work is in small samples of male competitive athletes.
- Add each obtained paper as its own record in `evidence/sources/` with
  `discovered_via: "Taylor 2002 ch. 5, ref N"`. That keeps the trail visible:
  anybody can see which of STAN's sources came from following this book up.
