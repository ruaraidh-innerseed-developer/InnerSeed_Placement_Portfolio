# Approaching a clinical lead

**You send these. Not STAN, not an assistant, not a template with your name on
it.** The whole reason this works is that a person who lived it is writing to a
person who studies it. Anything that smells of a mailshot goes in the bin.

---

## Why this is the unblocking move

Almost everything in the repository is waiting on one thing. No clinical lead
means nothing reaches `approved`, Tier B stays locked, and the flagship page stays
in draft. One name changes all of it.

## Who, and in what order

The author list on `grant-2023-endocrinologist-survey` is effectively the UK
shortlist, because they published the paper that documents the gap STAN exists to
fill: **Grant B, Pradeep A, Minhas S, Dhillo WS, Quinton R, Jayasena CN**,
*Reproduction & Fertility*, 2023, doi:10.1530/RAF-22-0097.

**1. Professor Channa Jayasena** (Imperial College London). Reproductive
endocrinology and andrology. Lead author on the 2022 Society for Endocrinology
testosterone guidelines *and* an active researcher into AAS withdrawal. The single
best fit in the country.

**2. Professor Richard Quinton** (Newcastle). Male hypogonadism, co-author on both
the survey and the guidelines. Known for engaging publicly on this.

**3. Bonnie Grant.** First author on the survey. Likely earlier-career, likely more
reachable, and first authors are usually the ones who care most about the topic.
Do not treat this as the fallback option — it may well be the best one.

**4. Mr Suks Minhas** (Imperial). Urology and andrology — the specialty that
actually sees AAS-related presentations.

Send to **one at a time**, in that order, a fortnight apart. Never round-robin the
same group; they know each other and it will show.

## The email

Short. Specific. A small ask. Sent from a real address with your real name.

> **Subject:** Patient-facing resource on steroid-induced hypogonadism — would you
> tear it apart?
>
> Dear Professor Jayasena,
>
> Your 2023 survey in *Reproduction & Fertility* on how endocrinologists manage
> recovery from anabolic steroid induced hypogonadism is why I'm writing.
>
> I used anabolic steroids for ten years. Coming off, I ran into what your paper
> describes, from the patient side: no pathway, and a great deal of confident
> nonsense filling the space where one should be. I've spent the time since
> reading my way out of it.
>
> I'm building a free, non-profit resource for men in that position. Plain-English
> explanations anchored to published guidelines and research, with a hard rule
> that we report what sources say and never advise, and that nothing clinical
> publishes without review by someone qualified. One page is drafted so far:
> [link].
>
> I'm not asking you to endorse anything, or to take on a role. I'd like twenty
> minutes of your honest view on two things: whether this is worth doing at all,
> and whether that page is safe as written. If it isn't, I would much rather know
> now than after a thousand people have read it.
>
> If it turns out to be useful, it would in time offer a route to a population
> that is notoriously hard to reach — for study recruitment, or for public
> engagement.
>
> With thanks for your time,
> [Your name]

## Why it is written that way

- **It opens with their work, accurately**, and does not tell them about their own
  field.
- **It says what you are in one line**, without a paragraph of biography. Ten years
  of use is the most compelling credential in the email; it does not need dressing.
- **The ask is twenty minutes and an opinion.** Not a role, not a board seat, not a
  contract. Small asks get answered; large ones get thought about and forgotten.
- **"I would rather know now"** removes the thing they are wary of — that you want
  validation rather than scrutiny. Give them permission to say it's bad and they
  are far more likely to engage.
- **It offers something real.** Recruitment access and public engagement are
  genuine currency in UK academia, and a hard-to-reach patient population is
  genuinely valuable. One sentence, no overselling.
- **No attachments, no deck, no logo.** One link.

## Before you send

1. The linked page must be live and readable on a real URL. A private link or a
   Google Doc undercuts the whole thing.
2. Re-read `content/pages/shbg.md` as if you were them, hunting for anything that
   drifts into advice. That is the thing that would lose you the reply.
3. Have an answer ready for "who else is involved?" — the honest one, "nobody yet,
   that's why I'm writing", is fine and better than implying a team.
4. Use a STAN address, not a personal one.

## If they reply

Say yes to whatever they offer, even if it is smaller than you hoped. A
twenty-minute call where a professor tells you the page is *mostly* fine is worth
more than a month of building. Take notes, act on every criticism, and send a
short follow-up showing what you changed. That second email is what converts an
opinion into a relationship.

## If they don't

Two weeks, then the next name. No chaser to the first — a reminder to a professor
who chose not to reply converts a maybe into a no.

Four silences means the email is wrong, not that the field is uninterested.
Rewrite it before spending the fifth name.

## Widen the net at the same time

These are not the only door, and academics are not the only useful reviewers:

- **Specialist pharmacists** — often deeply knowledgeable, far more reachable, and
  registered with the GPhC, which satisfies `EVIDENCE-POLICY.md` §8.
- **Clinical nurse specialists** in sexual health or andrology.
- **Needle and syringe programme staff** — they work with this population daily and
  are used to non-judgemental harm reduction.
- **Jim McVeigh** (Manchester Metropolitan) and the UK IPED research community —
  under-resourced, and interested in exactly this.

A GPhC-registered pharmacist willing to review content unlocks the register just as
effectively as a professor, and will probably answer faster.
