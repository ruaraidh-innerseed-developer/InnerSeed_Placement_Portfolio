# Editorial voice — how STAN writes

`SCOPE.md` says what STAN may publish. `INTERIM-STANDARD.md` says who has to
approve it. This says **how it is written**, which is the part that decides
whether anyone believes it.

The audience has been lied to by supplement companies, lectured by public
health campaigns, and dismissed by clinicians. They can smell all three. A
piece that reads as any of them is closed before the second paragraph.

---

## 1. The three questions

Almost every dispute in this field collapses because three separate questions
get answered with one answer.

| Question | What settles it |
|---|---|
| **Is it true?** | Does the thing itself hold. A molecular structure, a receptor count, a measured change. |
| **Is the consequence proven?** | Does the effect people infer from it actually follow, tested in humans. |
| **Is it safe?** | A wholly separate question that neither of the first two touches. |

The locker room answers all three with the first. Something is structurally
true, therefore the effect people expect from it follows, therefore doing it is
fine. Each arrow in that chain is a leap, and each one is where STAN does its
work.

**True is not proven. Proven is not safe.** Write it so plainly that a reader
who takes nothing else away takes that.

## 2. What STAN is not doing

**Not fighting everything.** Most of what circulates in the community is either
correct or harmless. A site that contradicts all of it is a site nobody
finishes reading, and it would be dishonest anyway.

**Not debunking.** The register is not a scoreboard. STAN is not scoring points
off people who were doing their best with the information they had.

**Not moralising.** "Steroids are bad" is not a sentence STAN writes. It closes
the conversation and it is not STAN's call to make.

**Not pretending they don't work.** They do, and the evidence says so. A
translator that will not concede the obvious is not trusted on anything else.
Taylor concedes it in the same chapter in which he dismantles the regimens, and
that is precisely why the dismantling lands.

The job is narrower and more useful than any of those: **find the specific link
in the chain that has not been tested, and name it.**

## 3. The worked example

From Taylor 2002, chapter 5, page 63. Three sentences, in order, and the whole
problem in miniature.

**First, the true part.** Stanozolol has a molecular alteration that makes it
non-aromatizable. MENT has a structure creating steric hindrance and is not
reduced by 5-alpha reductase. Both referenced. Both hold.
→ `stanozolol-non-aromatizable-by-structure`, `ment-steric-hindrance-not-5ar-reduced`

**Second, the plausible inference.** Alternating compounds that behave
differently might therefore potentiate their effects. Reasonable. Follows from
the first part the way most locker-room reasoning does, which is to say it
sounds right.

**Third, the sentence everyone drops.** *"However, there is no direct scientific
evidence to support this practice."*
→ `no-evidence-alternating-steroids-potentiates`

Nobody in a gym is lying. They have the molecular fact, which is real, and they
have the inference, which is sensible. They are missing the third sentence,
because the third sentence is boring and it is buried in a book.

**STAN's whole contribution is the third sentence.** Not contradicting the
first two. Supplying the one that got lost.

And then the separate question the passage never raises: stanozolol not
aromatising says nothing whatever about its effect on the liver or on lipids,
which is where the actual problem with that compound sits. True, unproven, and
unsafe are three different things and all three are live in one paragraph.

## 4. Where the leap usually happens

Five patterns cover most of it. Name the one in play rather than saying "there
is no evidence", which tells the reader nothing about where the reasoning broke.

**Mechanism to outcome.** It binds differently, so it must work better. A
receptor finding is not a result in a person.

**Animal to human.** The evidence for stacking multiple compounds comes from
livestock. There are no human studies. That is not a small caveat; it is the
whole finding.
→ `stacking-evidence-comes-from-livestock`

**Short study to long-term use.** The trials showing that steroids build muscle
ran 4 to 24 weeks, in men who were healthy at the start. Real use runs years,
in men who often are not.
→ `supratherapeutic-doses-increase-mass-and-strength`

**Works to safe.** The most common and the most costly. Effectiveness and
safety are measured differently, by different studies, and one never implies
the other.

**One marker to the whole picture.** Blood pressure, lipoproteins and liver
enzymes returning to normal is not the hormonal axis returning to normal, and a
tired man reading it as reassurance is the exact harm STAN exists to prevent.
→ `gains-largely-retained-three-months-after-stopping`

## 5. How to write about the last guy

He is not stupid and he is not the enemy. He is usually somebody a few years
further in who is trying to help, passing on what worked for him, in the total
absence of anyone qualified who would engage with the question.

So: **question the claim, never the person.** "This is where that reasoning
runs out" rather than "this is a myth". The reader has probably repeated it
themselves, and a person who feels caught out stops reading.

Where a belief is widely held, say so without sneering. It is evidence about
the information environment, not about the people in it.

And where the community is right, say that too, unprompted. It buys the credit
that the corrections spend.

## 6. Sentence level

- **Give the true part first, properly**, not as a concession before the
  correction. If the reader senses a "but" coming, the first half does not
  count.
- **Name the missing evidence specifically.** Not "more research is needed" but
  "nobody has tested this in humans; the studies are in cattle".
- **Never strengthen a source.** If it says "may", STAN says may. If it says
  "unproven", STAN says unproven. The temptation is always to tidy a hedge away.
- **Date the evidence.** "As things stood in 2002" is not a weakness, it is a
  prompt. Twenty-four-year-old absence of evidence is a research question, not
  a finding.
- **No dosing, ever**, including in the course of explaining why something does
  not work. `SCOPE.md`.
- **Say when STAN does not know.** The unwritten pages already do this and it is
  the most-praised thing about the site. It applies to sentences too.
- **Digestible, and awake.** Short sentences. One idea each. The aim is a reader
  who thinks "I had never thought to ask that" rather than one who feels
  corrected.

## 7. On Taylor specifically

He is a physician, the book is referenced throughout, and his angle is sound.
STAN's disagreements with it are about age, not about rigour: the book is from
2002 and the clinical literature has moved, particularly on cardiovascular risk
and on recovery of the hormonal axis.

Treat him as a colleague who did this job well twenty-four years ago. Where he
says the evidence is absent, check whether it still is before repeating it.
Where he hedges, keep the hedge. Where he says something is unproven and the
community has since decided otherwise, that gap is the article.

He also does something worth copying outright: every section separates **what
users do** from **what the science supports**, under two headings, on the same
page. That structure is the editorial voice made visible, and STAN should steal
it.
