# The sentence that gets left out

Somebody in the gym tells you stanozolol doesn't convert to oestrogen.

He's right. It doesn't. Stanozolol has a molecular alteration that makes it
non-aromatizable, which means the enzyme that would normally turn a steroid
into oestrogen can't get purchase on it. That isn't gym talk. It's in the
pharmacology, it's referenced, and a physician writing a textbook on this
subject states it plainly.

So far, so true.

Then comes the part that follows naturally. If different compounds behave
differently in the body, alternating between them ought to keep the body
guessing and make each one work better than it would on its own. That's a
reasonable thought. It isn't stupid, and it isn't a myth. It follows from the
true thing the way most gym reasoning follows from most true things.

Here's the sentence that goes missing.

> "However, there is no direct scientific evidence to support this practice."

That's from the same page of the same book, immediately after the molecular
facts. The author sets out why alternating might work, and then says nobody has
shown that it does.

Nobody in that conversation was lying to you. They had the first two sentences,
which are real and sensible. They were missing the third, because the third is
dull and it lives in a book rather than on a forum.

## Three questions, not one

It's worth separating what usually arrives fused together.

**Is it true?** Does the thing itself hold up. Stanozolol not aromatising: yes.

**Does the consequence follow?** Does the effect people expect from it actually
happen, tested in people. Alternating making compounds work better: not shown.

**Is it safe?** A completely separate question that neither of the others
touches.

That third one is worth sitting with. Stanozolol not converting to oestrogen
tells you nothing at all about what it does to your liver, or to your
cholesterol, which is where the real trouble with that compound tends to be.
"It doesn't aromatise" is true and it is not reassurance. It answers a
different question from the one worth asking.

## The one about cattle

Stacking gets the same treatment, and harder.

Stacking several compounds together might have additive effects. It might have
more than additive effects. It's plausible. And as at 2002, there were no human
medical studies investigating it at all. The scientific evidence people cite
for stacking comes from research on livestock.

Not a small caveat. That is the entire evidence base.

That's worth knowing before you decide anything, and it isn't a reason to think
less of anyone who stacks. The research doesn't exist. That's not their failing.

## What this isn't

This isn't a page telling you that everything you've heard is wrong. Most of it
isn't. The big one is true: steroids work. Studies in trained, healthy men show
real gains in muscle and strength, beyond what the training alone produces. A
site that wouldn't say that plainly wouldn't be worth reading on anything else.

Those studies ran between four and twenty-four weeks, in men who were healthy
when they started. Which is a different situation from years of use. But the
core of it stands, and pretending otherwise helps nobody.

## The habit worth picking up

Next time something gets passed to you as fact, try splitting it:

- What part of this is the actual finding?
- What part is what someone reckons follows from it?
- Has anyone tested the second part in a person?
- And separately, entirely separately, is it safe?

Usually the first part is solid. Usually the second part is untested. Usually
nobody asked the fourth at all.

You don't need a biochemistry degree to do that. You need to notice when a
sentence stops early.
