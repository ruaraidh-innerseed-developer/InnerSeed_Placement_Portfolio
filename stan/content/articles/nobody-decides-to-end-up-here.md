# Nobody decides to end up here

Nobody starts out planning to be on for six years.

It starts as a decision with an end date. One compound, a set number of weeks,
then a break. That is how almost everyone begins, and by its own terms it
works. Men who were already training put on real muscle and real strength, more
than the training alone was giving them. That is not gym talk, it is in the
research, and any site that will not say so plainly is not worth reading on
anything else.

Then the break comes.

## The break is where it turns

Some of the strength goes. The weights that moved easily stop moving easily.
Training feels heavier for less. Small injuries that used to clear up in a week
hang around. And a lot of people feel flat in a way they were not expecting and
had not been warned about.

Writing in 2002, a physician describing this pattern put it plainly: during the
break, first-time users lose some of what they gained even though they keep
training, workout intensity drops, minor injuries heal more slowly, and many
experience some level of depression. Then the sentence that matters most:

> "They become impatient for the next cycle, and often cut short their rest
> periods."

That is the first step, and it is a completely reasonable one. You feel worse.
The thing that made you feel better is sitting in a drawer. Nobody explained
that the dip was coming or how long it lasts. Shortening the break is not a
character failure. It is what a person does when they feel bad and have an
obvious lever.

## Then the same dose stops working

The next step is not a decision either.

Anabolic steroid use has been shown to increase the number of nuclei inside
muscle cells, and the number of androgen receptors inside those nuclei. More
receptors means more places for the steroid to bind. The proposal is that the
same amount, spread across more receptors, no longer does what it did.

The author who set this out was careful about it, and it is worth keeping his
care rather than tidying it away. His words were that this is *likely* to be
the reason increases are needed, and that "this theory needs further
investigation". So: a plausible mechanism, not a settled fact. We are telling
you the reasoning, not selling it to you.

But whatever the mechanism, the experience is real and near-universal. The
thing that worked stops working as well. And there is an obvious response to
that, and everyone knows what it is.

## What it looks like after a few years

Here is the same author, describing where the pattern goes:

> "Cycles become long periods of anabolic steroid use with little or no rest
> periods. In some users, rest periods become short periods of lower anabolic
> steroid use."

Read that second sentence again. The break has not been abandoned. It is still
called a break. It has just quietly become a period of less rather than a
period of none.

Alongside it, other things get added. Drugs believed to make the steroids work
better. Drugs to manage the effects the steroids are causing. He writes that
some heavy users are taking a dozen or more different things at the same time,
often obtained without any medical supervision at all.

At no point did anyone sit down and choose this. Every single step was a
sensible response to the step before it.

He also writes that over half of first-time users go on to a fifth cycle and
beyond with no real plans of quitting. He does not say where that figure comes
from and we have not traced it yet, so treat it as his assertion rather than a
statistic. But if you are reading this thinking you are the only one who
somehow never got off, you are not, and you are not close to being the only
one.

## The part nobody wants to say

This is the bit we could soften and are not going to.

For someone who has been using heavily for a long time, the same author writes
that stopping abruptly can have severe consequences. He writes, in the very
next sentence, that carrying on can too.

Both sentences. Together. That is the actual position, and it is why this is
not a problem you can think your way out of alone at two in the morning. There
is no version of this where the right answer is obvious and free.

What that means practically is not "keep going" and it is not "stop today". It
means this is the point where you need somebody qualified, and it is worth
knowing that finding one is genuinely hard rather than assuming you have looked
in the wrong place. Endocrinologists have no agreed approach to this. That is
not a rumour, it is a finding in a 2023 survey of them.

He finishes the section by describing people who have realised their
experimentation has become, in his words, a nightmare with no end in sight.

We are quoting that because if it is how you feel, you should know that a
doctor wrote it down in a book twenty-four years ago. It is a known, documented
place that people get to. It is not evidence of something wrong with you, and
it is not a verdict on where this finishes.

## What we can actually do

Not much on our own, and we would rather say that than pretend.

We can help you work out what to ask and who to ask it of. We can help you turn
what is happening into something a GP can act on rather than dismiss, which is
mostly a matter of walking in with the right words and the right numbers. We
can put you in a room with people at the same point, which does more than it
sounds like it does. And we are building a list of clinicians who understand
this population and will not lecture you, because the reason most people give
up on getting help is the first appointment, not the second.

What we cannot do is tell you how to come off. Not because of caution, but
because we are not qualified to and the honest answer is that it depends on
things only a clinician can assess.

**If tonight is bad, that is a different conversation and a more urgent one.**
The numbers at the bottom of this page are open now, and the people on them are
better at that than we are.

**Where this comes from.** William N. Taylor, *Anabolic Steroids and the

Fertility*, 2023.

**What we don't know.** Taylor's descriptions of what users experience are his
characterisations, not measured findings, and they describe the 1980s and
1990s. Nobody has counted how many people follow this pattern or how long it
takes. The mechanism for escalation is a proposal the author himself said
needed more work. We have not found a current source on the mood effects of
coming off, which is a real gap and one we would like to close.
