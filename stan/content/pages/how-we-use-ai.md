# How we use AI

## The short version

You have probably already put your symptoms or your blood results into an AI
assistant. Almost everyone does now, and we are not going to pretend that is a
bad instinct.

We use AI too. These pages are drafted with it.

**The difference is not whether a machine was involved. It is what happens
between the draft and the moment you read it.**

---

## "Why not just ask ChatGPT?"

Ask it. Genuinely. It is free, it is patient, it does not judge you, it is awake
at three in the morning, and it will explain the same thing six times without
sighing. For a lot of questions that is exactly what you need, and there is no
reason to be precious about it.

But for *this* subject specifically, there are four things a general assistant
cannot do, and all four matter.

**It cannot tell you when the answer does not exist.** This is the big one. On
recovery after long-term steroid use, the honest answer to many questions is "the
research has not been done." A general model will usually produce a confident,
fluent, plausible answer anyway. Confidence is not the same as evidence, and in
this field the gap between them is unusually wide.

**It does not know whether the study fits you.** Most testosterone research is
about men in their sixties whose levels declined naturally. You may be thirty-two
and four years off 400mg a week. Those are different people with different
physiology and different outlooks, and an answer built from the first group and
handed to the second is wrong in a way that sounds completely right.

**It cannot show its working.** You cannot click through, see which study, check
what population it was in, or see when anyone last looked at it.

**It cannot be corrected and stay corrected.** If it gets something wrong today,
there is nobody to tell, and it will say the same thing to the next person.

## "Do you use AI to write this?"

Yes. Openly.

Drafts here are produced with AI assistance, and we would rather say so than let
you assume otherwise. A great deal of health content online is now written this
way and does not admit it. We think telling you is the more trustworthy move, not
the more embarrassing one.

## "So what's the difference?"

What happens to the draft.

**Every claim is tied to a named source, and that source is on the record.** We
keep a register of everything we cite. Each entry records what the source may be
used to claim — and, just as importantly, what it must **not** be used to claim.
Nobody gets to stretch a study about older men into a statement about you.

**We record what we have not read.** Sources sit in the register marked unread and
unverified until someone has actually gone through the full text. You can see that
state. A page that leans on something unread says so.

**We refuse figures we cannot stand behind.** There is a recovery timeline in
circulation that we are deliberately not publishing, because we have not yet
checked which population it came from. A chatbot asked the same question will
quote it to you today.

**Pages are labelled with where they are up to.** Reviewed, drafted, or not
written yet. Nothing pretends to be finished when it is not.

**Someone's name is on it.** A person wrote it, a second person read it, and once
we have a clinical lead a qualified person reviews it. If it is wrong, there is
somebody to tell, and it gets fixed for everyone.

**We say when we do not have something.** Search this site for a topic we have not
covered and it will tell you plainly, then point you at someone who does know,
rather than inventing something to fill the space.

None of that is exotic. It is just editing — the thing that used to sit between a
draft and a reader, and mostly does not any more.

## "What are you actually saving me?"

**Time.** You do not have to read forty papers or four hundred forum posts to work
out what LH is and why yours is on the floor.

**Money.** You should not have to pay a private clinic to explain what a binding
protein does. That part should be free, and here it is.

**Mistakes.** Most of the harm in this field is not from one dramatic error. It is
from small decisions made without knowing what they cost, or from walking into an
appointment unable to ask the question that would have changed it.

## "Can you promise this is safe?"

No. And be wary of anyone who does.

There is nothing natural about staying unnatural for long, and everything comes
with a side effect. That is true whether you are on, coming off, or being looked
after by the best clinician in the country. Nobody can hand you a guarantee. The
people offering you one are usually selling something.

What we can do is make sure you are not guessing — and put you next to people who
have been where you are, and people qualified to answer what we cannot.

That is not safety. It is not nothing, either.

## If you are going to ask an AI anyway — and you will

Here is how to get a less bad answer. This applies to any of them.

1. **Give it your real numbers, with the units and the reference range** from your
   own report. "My testosterone is low" gets you a generic answer. "Total
   testosterone 11.2 nmol/L, lab range 8.6–29, SHBG 62 nmol/L" gets you something
   you can take somewhere.
2. **Ask what population the evidence came from.** "Were these men my age with my
   history?" is the question that catches most bad answers.
3. **Ask what it is unsure about.** Then ask again, harder. Models will usually
   concede uncertainty if you actually invite it, and almost never volunteer it.
4. **Ask for the sources and then go and look at them.** Check the paper exists,
   check it says what you were told, check who was in it.
5. **Never let it tell you what to take.** Not because it will refuse, but because
   it does not know your history, cannot see your bloods, and carries none of the
   consequences.

## What we will not use AI for

- **Answering you personally.** When you contact STAN, a human replies. If that
  ever changes you will be told in plain language, up front.
- **Deciding what is true.** AI drafts. People decide what publishes.
- **Interpreting your results.** Not by us, and not by a tool on this site.
- **Pretending to be a person.** Anything automated here will say that it is.
