## The short version

Most of the testosterone in your blood is stuck to a carrier protein. While it is
stuck, your body cannot use it. That protein is SHBG.

So your report has two very different things on it that both look like
"testosterone": the **total**, which counts everything including the portion that
is locked away, and the **free**, which is the part actually available to do a job.

If your SHBG is high, a lot of your total is locked away. Your total can sit
comfortably inside the normal range while the usable amount is low. You feel bad,
and the letter says normal.

---

## "My SHBG is high. What does that mean?"

It means more of your testosterone is bound up and less of it is free.

Think of your total testosterone as money in an account and SHBG as the portion
that is locked in a fixed-term deposit. The balance looks healthy. What you can
actually spend this week might not be.

High SHBG does not itself make you unwell. What matters is the knock-on effect:
for any given total testosterone, a higher SHBG leaves a smaller free portion.

Two men can have exactly the same total testosterone, and if one has double the
SHBG of the other, they are in quite different situations. Nothing on the total
line tells you which man you are.

## "My SHBG is low. What does that mean?"

The opposite: less is bound, so more of your total is free. That can mean your
free testosterone is higher than the total alone would suggest.

Low SHBG is also a signal in its own right. It is commonly seen alongside insulin
resistance, type 2 diabetes and higher body weight, and it is a well-recognised
effect of androgens — particularly oral 17-alpha-alkylated compounds, which can
drive it down sharply. A low SHBG is often the thing on a panel that tells a
clinician something about the rest of your metabolic picture, not just your
hormones.

## "My testosterone is normal but I feel terrible. Is this why?"

It is one of the first things worth ruling out, and it is a common reason for this
exact experience.

UK guidance from the Society for Endocrinology addresses it directly: where SHBG
is abnormal, total testosterone does not reliably reflect what testosterone is
actually doing in the body, and a **calculated free testosterone** is the
appropriate measure.

Free testosterone is usually not measured directly. It is worked out from three
numbers you may already have on your report — **total testosterone**, **SHBG** and
**albumin** — using a formula, most commonly the Vermeulen equation. If all three
are on your panel, the calculation can be done.

If your total was inside the range and nobody looked at your SHBG, then the
question of what is actually available to your body has not yet been asked.

## "What number should mine be?"

Use the range printed on your own report.

Reference ranges for SHBG differ between laboratories — different equipment,
different populations, different cut-offs. A number that reads as high at one lab
can read as within range at another. Any figure quoted on the internet, including
on a page like this one, is less reliable than the range sitting next to your own
result.

What travels between labs is the *relationship*, not the number: higher SHBG means
less free testosterone for the same total. That is the part worth understanding,
and it holds wherever you were tested.

## "What makes SHBG go up or down?"

These are well-recognised associations, not a checklist of things to fix.

**Commonly raised by:** an overactive thyroid · liver disease · oestrogens ·
some anticonvulsant medicines · ageing · sustained caloric restriction and very
low body fat.

**Commonly lowered by:** obesity and insulin resistance · type 2 diabetes · an
underactive thyroid · androgens, especially oral 17-alpha-alkylated compounds ·
glucocorticoids · growth hormone.

Note that thyroid problems appear on both lists in opposite directions. This is
one reason a thyroid result is worth having alongside an SHBG that looks odd.

## When it's suppressed — and the surprise

Here is the part most pages will not tell you, because most pages are not written
for people who have used anabolic steroids.

Exogenous androgens usually push SHBG **down**. Orals push it down hard. So the
expected picture in someone with a history of heavy use is a *low* SHBG.

Which means a **high** SHBG in someone with that history is unexpected, and
unexpected findings are worth asking about rather than ignoring. It may point
somewhere other than the steroids — thyroid, liver, or something else entirely —
and it changes how the rest of the panel reads.

We are not going to tell you what your high SHBG means. We are telling you that it
is a legitimate thing to walk into an appointment and ask about, and that "your
testosterone is normal" is not an answer to it.

## What this number does not tell you

- **Whether you feel bad because of it.** SHBG is one number among several and
  symptoms have many causes.
- **What your free testosterone is**, on its own. The calculation needs total
  testosterone and albumin too.
- **Why it is what it is.** The lists above are associations. Working out which
  one applies to you, if any, is a clinical job.
- **Whether anything needs doing.** That is not a question a website can answer,
  and this page does not try.

## Questions worth asking

1. What is my SHBG, and what is the reference range at the lab that ran it?
2. Do we have total testosterone and albumin as well, so a **calculated free
   testosterone** can be worked out?
3. Given my SHBG, does my total testosterone reflect what is actually available?
4. My SHBG is higher than I would have expected given my history — is that worth
   looking into?
5. Should my thyroid be checked alongside this?

Our **consultation brief** puts these on a sheet with space for your own numbers.

## Related markers

**Total testosterone** — the number SHBG modifies the meaning of.
**Albumin** — the third figure the free testosterone calculation needs.
**Thyroid (TSH, free T4)** — moves SHBG in both directions.
**LH** — tells you where in the loop the problem sits.
