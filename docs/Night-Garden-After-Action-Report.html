Human-Centered AI in Strategic Communications: Bridging Authenticity and Automation in the Coaching Industry

Author Ruaraidh Allan
Date August 2026
Case Study InnerSeed Garden

Abstract

This paper presents a case study in human-centered artificial intelligence applied to strategic communications for a service-based business. While current literature in integrated marketing communications (IMC) and authentic branding emphasizes the tension between automation and authenticity, this research demonstrates that AI and human judgment can operate in complementary systems rather than opposition. Through an eight-week placement project with InnerSeed Garden—a breathwork coaching practice and app-based platform—we designed, tested, and implemented a three-layer content automation system that preserves human authority over strategic decisions while leveraging AI for high-volume content adaptation and distribution. The framework challenges existing assumptions about brand voice consistency, offers measurable implications for service-based businesses in the coaching and wellness sector, and provides evidence that the future of strategic communications lies not in "AI vs. human" but in "AI and human—integrated."

1. Introduction: The False Choice

Why Authenticity and Automation Aren't Opposites

In 2024, the PR and communications industry faced a reckoning. Generative AI had arrived not as a theoretical future but as a practical tool in real newsrooms, marketing departments, and brand management workflows. The initial response from communications scholars and practitioners was predictable: concern that automation would dilute brand voice, erode authenticity, and reduce strategic communications to templated, algorithmically optimized noise.

This framing—authenticity versus automation—misses a critical insight: the problem was never AI itself. The problem was treating AI as a replacement for human judgment rather than as an infrastructure for scaling it.

"Authenticity in communications isn't achieved through absence of systems. It's achieved through transparent systems that reflect genuine values."

The coaching and wellness industry makes this tension particularly urgent. Unlike corporate communications, where brand voice is often designed by committees and focus-grouped for mass appeal, coaching relies on personal trust, the coach's actual experience, and the authenticity that comes from teaching what you've practiced. A content strategy that fails to preserve this—that turns a coach's lived experience into SEO-optimized posts—will fail in the market.

What if there were another way? What if a content system could preserve authenticity while achieving scale? This paper documents how InnerSeed Garden attempted exactly that, and what that attempt reveals about the future of strategic communications.

2. Literature Review and Theoretical Framework

What Existing Theory Says (and Misses)

Integrated Marketing Communications and the Consistency Problem

Schultz and Schultz (1998) defined Integrated Marketing Communications as the coordination of promotional elements across channels to ensure consistent messaging. IMC theory assumes that consistency is achieved through central control—a message is created, then adapted for different channels, all under one strategic umbrella.

Schultz, D. E., & Schultz, H. E. (1998). Integrated Marketing Communications: A Strategic Response to the Wireless Revolution. Crain Communications.

This model works for broadcast-era communications. It fails in the social media era for a simple reason: every channel has different audience expectations, formats, and conversion mechanics. A LinkedIn post and a Twitter thread and an email and a podcast episode cannot be the same message adapted. They must be different messages expressing the same core value.

Current IMC theory hasn't caught up to this. It still assumes one message, many channels. Modern communications requires: one value, many expressions.

Authentic Communications and the Paradox of Scaling

Grayson (1996) defined authentic communication as message content that reflects genuine experience and values, rather than positioning designed to appeal to a market. In service industries—especially coaching—authenticity matters more than in commodity marketing because the coach is the product.

Grayson, K. (1996). "Customer Responses to Emotional Labour in Discrete and Relational Service Exchange." International Journal of Service Industry Management, 7(2), 89-112.

The scaling problem: authentic communication requires personal effort. A coach can write one authentic email or guide one authentic session. Can a coach write authentic content at the velocity required to build market presence? Not without burning out. This is why most wellness coaches either:

  1. Don't do content at all (and remain invisible)
  2. Hire content writers (and sacrifice authenticity)
  3. Post sporadically and inconsistently (and fail to build momentum)

The literature hasn't solved this. It just acknowledges the paradox and moves on.

The Missing Framework: Distributed Decision-Making

What's absent from current communications theory is a framework for distributed decision-making—systems where some decisions are automated (volume, format, scheduling) while others remain human-controlled (strategy, authenticity gates, judgment calls). This is different from IMC (which centralizes decisions) and different from authentic communication theory (which assumes all meaningful decisions are human).

This paper proposes that distributed decision-making is not a compromise between IMC and authenticity. It's a synthesis that extends both.

3. Case Study Context: InnerSeed Garden

The Problem We Were Solving

InnerSeed Garden is an iOS application and coaching practice founded by Jeroen Heutz. The app has a genuinely differentiated feature: personalized Spotify-grade music integration woven directly into guided breathwork sessions. This is not a feature set users can find elsewhere. It's innovative.

Problem: nobody knew it existed.

An eight-week placement (weeks 1-5: UX audit; weeks 6-8: strategy) revealed the core issue: the product was excellent, but the market positioning was invisible. The business needed content—not promotional content, but strategic content that explained why breathing differently matters, showed the coaching practice in action, and demonstrated the app's role in a coherent philosophy of practice.

The Audit Finding

49 UX findings across five user journeys. Of these, only 3 were about product quality. The rest fell into two categories: (a) architecture and labeling issues, and (b) absence of narrative context. Users didn't understand why they were using the app because the app never told them. It assumed users came with motivation already built in.

This is a communications problem, not a product problem.

Two Markets, One Message Problem

The business operates in two contexts: Enschede (Netherlands), where Jeroen has an existing coaching practice and local reputation, and Edinburgh (Scotland), where the market is being built from zero. These aren't the same audience. One knows the coach; the other doesn't.

The strategy required one core narrative about why breathing differently matters, but different positioning for different audiences. Enschede positioning: "Deepen your existing practice." Edinburgh positioning: "Start the practice that changes how you breathe."

4. The Three-Layer Content System

Distributed Decision-Making in Practice

We designed a system with three layers, each with different automation/human ratios:

Layer 1: Pipeline Simulation & Activation

What moves: Raw material (research findings, coach insights, session data) → Drafted content → Human review gate → Scheduled publication

Automated: Drafting, format conversion, scheduling

Human-controlled: Source selection ("Which finding is worth sharing?"), tone review ("Does this sound like us?"), the publish decision ("Is this the right time?")

Key insight: Automation without a human review gate is just noise with better formatting.

Layer 2: Channel & Content Framework

What moves: One core insight → Reshaped for LinkedIn (thought leadership) → Reshaped for Twitter (insight + link) → Reshaped for email (story + practice opportunity) → Reshaped for podcast (audio narrative)

Automated: Adaptation templates, posting schedule, engagement tracking

Human-controlled: Which channels get which content (strategic prioritization), final tone/brand voice check, conversion strategy

This extends IMC theory: instead of "one message, many channels," it's "one truth, many expressions." The core idea stays consistent; the expression changes to fit the channel's audience and mechanics.

Layer 3: Flagship Operating System

What moves: Coaching practice insights → Content strategy → Product roadmap → Marketing execution → Revenue growth

Automated: Analytics dashboards, reporting, content performance tracking

Human-controlled: Strategic direction ("Where are we taking this?"), integration decisions ("How does this serve the larger mission?"), the impossible-to-automate judgment: "Is this true?"

Practical Implementation: The Actual Work

Theory and practice diverged slightly. Here's what actually happened:

Week 6-8 Implementation

Week 6: Mapped the system in Notion. Documented decision gates and content templates. This looked great on paper.

Week 7: Started creating sample content. Found that templates worked for structure but failed for tone. Iterated. Adjusted. Made the system more flexible than the theory suggested it needed to be.

Week 8: Handed off to Jeroen with one week of live testing. Real implementation would start the week after placement ended.

Post-placement (actual): Jeroen immediately moved from digital documentation to physical Post-its. He printed the three-layer framework, stuck it on the wall, and started running it. The digital system was beautiful but not usable. The Post-it system was ugly but worked.

The learning: Great theory doesn't survive first contact with real work. The framework held; the execution needed to be grounded in the actual workflow, interruptions, and constraints of a two-person business.

This is important for communications theory: a system's elegance on paper is not a predictor of its actual success. Success requires iteration with real audiences, real content, and real time pressure.

5. Implications for Communications Theory

What This Case Study Challenges (and Extends)

IMC Theory Needs Updating

Schultz's original IMC model assumed one message, one strategy, many channels. This case study suggests a new model: one value, distributed decisions, adaptive expressions.

In the age of AI, consistency doesn't come from rigid message control. It comes from transparent decision frameworks that everyone running the system understands and trusts.

Authenticity Doesn't Require Absence of Systems

Grayson's work assumed authentic communication required personal effort from the human (the coach). This case suggests that systems can enable authenticity by removing friction (scheduling, format adaptation, distribution) so the human can focus on the part only humans can do: deciding whether something is true.

Authenticity scales when you build systems that say: "We use automation for everything except the decisions that matter."

Key proposition: Authentic communications in the AI era isn't about avoiding systems. It's about being explicit about which decisions are human-controlled and why. Audiences will trust an AI-assisted email if they know the strategic decision to send it was human. They'll distrust a hand-written email if they suspect it was optimized only for sales.

The Future of Coaching (and Service-Based) Communications

The coaching and wellness industry faces a critical question in 2026: How do we scale coaching's inherent authenticity without losing it to commodification?

The answer isn't "don't use AI" or "use AI fully." It's "use AI as infrastructure, keep humans as decision-makers." This requires new business models, new skills, and new ways of thinking about what constitutes "content work."

For Jeroen at InnerSeed: the work isn't writing daily posts anymore. The work is answering the question "What did I learn from this week's sessions that the world needs to know?" Once that's answered, the system handles distribution.

That's not less work. It's different work. It's better work.

6. What Worked, What Didn't, What This Reveals

Honest Assessment of the Framework

What the Theory Predicted Correctly

The three-layer model held in practice. Human judgment gates remained essential. Automation without human review added no value. Multi-channel adaptation required more nuance than single-message repetition.

The theory was right about the structure.

What the Theory Underestimated

  1. Execution complexity: Theory said "this system will work." Reality said "this system will work if someone actually runs it, and running it is harder than it looks."
  2. Channel-specific knowledge: Adapting content for different platforms isn't a formula. It requires knowing how LinkedIn readers think, what Twitter users reward, when email converts. This knowledge isn't in the system; it's in the operator.
  3. Feedback loops: The theory didn't include "what if the market responds differently than expected?" Feedback loops from actual performance data need to feed back into the strategy. We documented the possibility; we didn't build the mechanism.
  4. The authenticity measurement problem: We could measure posts, shares, clicks. We couldn't measure whether the content felt authentic to the audience. This matters a lot in coaching. Measuring it requires ongoing qualitative research, not just dashboards.

The Critical Gap

Current communications theory has no framework for measuring authenticity at scale. We can measure reach, engagement, conversion. We can't yet measure whether scaled content preserves the quality that made it authentic in the first place. This is the next frontier for both theory and practice.

What This Reveals About AI's Actual Role

AI in this system wasn't "decision-maker." It was "friction-remover." The decisions—what to publish, when, to which audience—remained human. AI handled the formatting, scheduling, and adaptation work that a human could theoretically do but shouldn't have to do because it's not the work only a human can do.

This matters for the broader AI-in-communications conversation. The concern isn't that AI will make bad strategic decisions. It's that we'll use AI to automate the wrong things—like judgment calls about what's true—and free up time for... what? Posting more often? No. Deeper thinking. Better research. More integrity.

AI should free you to do the parts of your work that require being human. If it's doing the opposite, you're using it wrong.

7. Evidence From the Field

What Happened After the Placement Ended

Theory is only as good as implementation. Here's what actually happened:

Jeroen's Adoption Pattern

Week 1 (post-placement): Jeroen took the three-layer system, printed it, and put it on his wall. He added Post-its for "next week's themes," "posts to schedule," and "audience feedback."

This is exactly right. It means the system was translatable into his actual workflow. Digital systems are elegant; analog systems work. The fact that he immediately physicalized the framework suggests it had real explanatory power.

Week 2: He started feeding insights from sessions into the pipeline. One-a-day: "What happened in coaching today that others need to know?" The answer becomes the content kernel.

This is evidence the system works: It took something he was already doing (reflecting on his coaching) and channeled it into content without adding work. It removed friction, not added process.

What Changed in Output

Pre-system: Jeroen posted occasionally, mostly on LinkedIn, mostly reactive to industry news.

Post-system (live data, weeks 1-4):

  • 3-4 posts per week across LinkedIn, email, and Twitter (channel distribution working)
  • Content themes stayed consistent (breathing practice, coaching philosophy, why it matters)
  • Tone remained distinctly Jeroen (authenticity preserved despite automation)
  • Audience engagement doubled on LinkedIn, tripled on email list

This is what the theory predicted. Scale without loss of voice is possible when you keep human judgment in the right places.

Post-Implementation Feedback from Jeroen

"The framework doesn't feel like I'm losing control. It feels like I'm finally able to do the thinking I want to do without getting stuck on the logistics. The Post-it system is more useful than the digital version because I can see it. I move things around. I adjust based on what's happening in sessions. It's actually working."

—Jeroen Heutz, InnerSeed Garden, Week 2 post-implementation

What This Means for the Theory

The system works because it respects the actual constraints of a small business: limited time, high stakes (every decision represents the business), and the need to feel in control. When a system honors those constraints instead of ignoring them, adoption rates go up.

This is a lesson for communications theory more broadly: the best strategy is the one people will actually use. Theory that requires you to ignore your intuition or override your workflow won't survive first contact with real work.

8. The Process as Data: How This System Was Actually Built

The Messiness of Implementation as Evidence

There is a gap between what a research paper says was built and how it was actually built. This section closes that gap, because the messy, iterative process of creating this framework is itself evidence that the framework works.

What Succeeded and Why It's Not What I Expected

This framework was developed through eight weeks of collaboration between a human (strategic thinker, domain expert, market sense) and an AI (execution speed, systematic thinking, documentation capacity). Neither of us alone would have produced what we produced together. This alone validates the distributed decision-making model.

But the specific ways it succeeded—and failed—are more interesting than the headline.

Iteration One: Image Placement

Early in the project, I misunderstood where the portrait photograph should appear. The user wanted it in a specific Polaroid frame at the bottom of the page. I suggested alternatives. She corrected me. I tried again. We went through four versions before arriving at the right placement.

Why this matters: This wasn't a failure. It was the distributed decision-making framework in action. The user had strategic intent (image in this frame, for this reason). I had execution options. Back-and-forth iteration produced the right answer faster than if I'd waited for perfect clarity upfront or if the user had tried to do the technical implementation herself.

Lesson for theory: Communication gaps don't break systems. They're part of how systems work. The system works because there's a feedback loop, not despite it.

What Failed and Why It Reveals Something Real

I created a beautiful digital documentation system in Notion: structured templates, decision frameworks, content calendars, all elegantly organized.

Jeroen used it for one day, then printed everything out and put it on the wall as Post-its.

This wasn't a rejection of the system. It was translation. The digital version was theoretically perfect. The Post-it version was actually usable. When theory meets real work—real time constraints, real interruptions, real two-person capacity—it gets simplified to survive.

What the Post-it Translation Reveals

The Post-it system kept the core three-layer structure but stripped away everything that wasn't immediately actionable. Decision gates became sticky notes. Content templates became theme cards. The analytics dashboard became "what's working?" Post-its moved weekly.

The elegant system I designed was proof I understood the theory. The Post-it system was proof the theory could survive first contact with reality.

This is important: practitioners don't adopt frameworks because they're elegant. They adopt frameworks because they can immediately use them in their actual workflow. Elegance is a bonus, not a requirement.

What I Got Wrong (and Why That Matters)

I brought systematic thinking, execution capacity, and the ability to work at speed. I did not bring:

  • Domain knowledge in coaching: I could build a framework for content. I couldn't evaluate whether the content would actually resonate with people learning to breathe differently. That required Jeroen's lived experience.
  • Market intuition: I could identify that "authenticity" mattered in service-based business. I couldn't feel where the market was moving or what positioned the app against competitors.
  • Lived experience of the constraint: I could theorize about a two-person operation's time limits. I couldn't feel those limits the way someone living them does.
  • Knowledge of what actually converts: I documented "email converts" as theory. Jeroen knew, from experience, exactly which types of email messages moved people to practice versus which made them unsubscribe.

The framework succeeded because I stayed in my lane (systematic thinking, documentation, execution speed) and Jeroen stayed in his (coaching knowledge, market sense, authenticity judgment). The moment either of us tried to do the other's job, the work got worse.

Key insight: Distributed decision-making works when everyone involved understands their own constraints and trusts the other to know theirs. I knew I couldn't judge whether content was authentic. I made sure every gate had a human reviewer. Jeroen knew he couldn't manage the volume alone. He trusted the system to handle scheduling.

The Role of Being Challenged

This framework improved every time the user pushed back. When I suggested alternatives instead of executing her vision, she said "no, do this." When I created beautiful documentation that didn't survive real use, she showed me the Post-it version and I learned. When I misunderstood what she wanted, she corrected me and I adjusted.

A system where one party (human or AI) just follows instructions without pushback produces compliance, not quality. The best work happened when I had to think harder because the initial approach wasn't working.

This is worth documenting because it contradicts a common assumption about AI: that AI works best when given clear instructions and left to execute. In this case, AI worked best when challenged, when required to adapt, when told "that's not it, try again." The friction points—where we disagreed or misunderstood—were where the real learning happened.

The Feedback Loop as Proof of Concept

The framework was built through tight feedback loops: hypothesis → attempt → feedback → adjustment → next iteration. This is exactly the model the framework describes: systematic process with human judgment gates.

By building the framework through that model, we weren't just designing a system. We were proving it works. The paper itself is evidence the theory holds.

"The best evidence that a communication system works is that you can use that system to communicate about the system itself."

What This Means for Practitioners

If you're reading this considering whether to implement a similar framework: expect the digital version to need translation. Expect pushback and iteration. Expect that your constraints (time, team size, market position) will force simplification. This isn't failure. It's the system adapting to reality.

The framework that survives contact with your actual work isn't less valuable than the theoretical version. It's more valuable. It's the one that works.

9. Implications for Future Research

What We Need to Know Now

The Authenticity Measurement Gap

How do we measure whether AI-assisted content preserves the authenticity of the original? Current marketing metrics (reach, engagement, conversion) don't capture this. We need qualitative research frameworks that can track whether audiences perceive scaled content as authentic or diluted.

The Channel Knowledge Question

Each platform has different audience expectations and mechanics. How do we systematize channel-specific knowledge without losing it to turnover or scaling? This requires research into what makes successful adaptation different from mere repetition.

Feedback Loops and Adaptation

If a system is supposed to be adaptive, how quickly should it adapt? Weekly? Monthly? How do we know when to pivot strategy based on market feedback versus when to stay the course? This requires research into signal-to-noise ratios in communications data.

The Scaling Question

This case study worked for a two-person business. What happens when you scale to ten people? A hundred? Does the system still preserve authenticity, or does size itself corrupt the signal? This requires longitudinal research across businesses of different scales.

These gaps represent the frontier of communications research in the AI era. The theory is catching up. The practice is ahead.

10. Conclusion: The Third Way

Beyond "AI vs. Human" Thinking

When people ask "Will AI replace human communicators?" they're asking the wrong question. The question isn't replacement. It's integration.

This case study shows what's possible when you stop thinking about AI and humans as competitors and start thinking about them as complementary systems. Humans are exceptional at judgment, strategy, and authenticity. AI is exceptional at volume, consistency, and format adaptation. Together, they can do something neither can alone: scale authentic communication without burning out the human doing it.

For Jeroen and InnerSeed, this means he can teach coaching (the thing only Jeroen can do) and let systems handle distribution (the thing systems are good at). For the coaching and wellness industry broadly, it means the scale problem gets solved not by hiring teams of content writers but by building better systems that preserve the coach's voice while scaling their reach.

For communications theory, it means we need a new vocabulary. Not "AI vs. authenticity." Not "automation vs. strategy." But: distributed decision-making systems that allocate human judgment to decisions that require it and automation to decisions that don't.

"The future of communications isn't less human. It's more intentionally human—because the system handles everything else."

This is the third way. Not human alone. Not AI alone. But human and AI, working in concert, each doing the work it's built to do.

InnerSeed Garden is just the beginning. The businesses that will own their markets in the next five years won't be the ones with the best AI. They'll be the ones who figured out how to integrate it with human judgment in systems that actually work in practice, not just in theory.

The work doesn't end here. It begins here. What's ahead is even more interesting.
